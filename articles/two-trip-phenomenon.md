## Abstract

Transporting groceries from a vehicle to a household appears to be a straightforward logistical task.

A number of bags must be moved from one location to another.

The number of required trips should therefore be determined by the number of bags and the amount that can be carried at once.

In practice, however, individuals frequently announce that the groceries can be brought inside in one trip, or perhaps two, before discovering that an additional trip is necessary.

The present study examines this discrepancy.

Observations suggest that the expected number of trips is influenced by confidence, reluctance to return outside, and a tendency to treat carrying capacity as a negotiable property.

The authors conclude that the final trip is often not included in the original estimate because its contents have not yet been noticed.

## Introduction

Groceries begin outside.

They are required inside.

Between these two conditions lies the process of carrying them.

The task is commonly approached with the intention of minimising the number of trips.

This intention is reasonable.

Returning to the vehicle requires walking.

Walking takes time.

The vehicle may be some distance away.

It may also be raining.

Consequently, individuals often attempt to transport as many bags as possible at once.

The statement "I can get all of this in one trip" is generally made before the bags have been lifted.

The present study investigates what happens next.

## Methodology

Ordinary grocery-unloading situations were considered under conditions approximating everyday household activity.

The procedure began when an individual inspected a collection of groceries and estimated how many trips would be necessary to bring them indoors.

The individual was then permitted to carry the groceries using whatever arrangement appeared reasonable at the time.

The following behaviours were examined:

- placing several bag handles over the same fingers;
- transferring bags between hands to improve balance;
- carrying an item under one arm while holding bags in both hands;
- attempting to close a vehicle door without putting anything down;
- announcing that the remaining items would require "just one more trip";
- and returning to the vehicle after all groceries were believed to have been collected.

Particular attention was given to the difference between a trip described as the last trip and a trip that was actually the last trip.

No attempt was made to establish a universal safe carrying limit.

This was considered important because the participants appeared unwilling to recognise one.

## Results

The number of trips initially predicted was frequently lower than the number eventually undertaken.

Several recurring explanations were identified.

### The Maximum Capacity Assumption

Participants tended to estimate their carrying capacity using the greatest number of bags they could imagine holding.

This estimate did not consistently account for the weight of the bags.

Three bags containing bread were treated as equivalent to three bags containing bottles.

They were not equivalent.

Nevertheless, the initial plan often remained unchanged until the bags were lifted.

At this point, a revised plan became necessary.

The revised plan was generally described as the original plan with a small adjustment.

### The Additional Bag Principle

A notable feature of grocery transport was the assumption that one additional bag could always be carried.

When both hands were occupied, participants sometimes located a finger that appeared not to be contributing sufficiently.

Another bag was assigned to that finger.

If the arrangement remained stable, the individual considered it successful.

If the arrangement became uncomfortable, the individual considered it temporarily successful.

This distinction allowed the transport operation to continue beyond the point at which an uninvolved observer might have recommended a second trip.

### The Doorway Constraint

A carrying arrangement that worked beside the vehicle did not necessarily work at the front door.

Keys had to be located.

Handles had to be operated.

Doors had to be opened.

Some doors required a hand.

The availability of hands had not always been included in the original carrying calculation.

As a result, participants sometimes reached the doorway with all the groceries they could carry and no practical means of entering the building.

The groceries were then placed on the ground.

This was recorded as an intermediate unloading event, although it had not been part of the plan.

### The Forgotten Item

Even when the estimated number of trips appeared sufficient, a final item could remain in the vehicle.

Common examples included a small bag placed behind a seat, a bulky item not packed in a bag, or a single object that had rolled out of view.

The individual might already have announced that the unloading was finished.

Discovery of the remaining item required another trip.

This trip was typically described as unnecessary.

The item, however, still needed to come inside.

## Discussion

The findings suggest that the Two-Trip Phenomenon is not simply a problem of arithmetic.

If a person has eight bags and can comfortably carry four, two trips would appear sufficient.

However, this calculation assumes that all eight bags are visible, equally portable, and ready to be carried.

It also assumes that the person can open the door.

In everyday conditions, these assumptions may not hold.

The study therefore proposes a distinction between **theoretical carrying capacity** and **operational carrying capacity**.

Theoretical carrying capacity is the number of bags a person believes they can carry while standing beside a vehicle.

Operational carrying capacity is the number they can carry while walking, locating their keys, opening a door, and maintaining the contents of the bags in approximately their original condition.

The two values are not necessarily equal.

A second distinction concerns the phrase "one more trip."

This phrase appears to describe a numerical quantity.

In practice, it may function as an expression of hope.

A person saying "one more trip" is not always reporting that precisely one trip remains.

They may instead be expressing a desire not to discuss the possibility of further trips.

The phrase is therefore best understood as a provisional forecast.

It should not be treated as a guarantee.

## Limitations

The present study does not establish that every grocery-unloading task requires an additional trip.

Some individuals accurately estimate their carrying capacity.

Others deliberately make several comfortable trips.

A small number may bring everything inside at once.

The authors acknowledge these possibilities.

They also note that the phenomenon may be affected by distance, weather, bag design, door configuration, and the presence of another person who has offered to help but is not currently helping.

Further investigation would be required to determine whether the last remaining bag is more likely to be discovered after someone has removed their shoes.

## Conclusion

The Two-Trip Phenomenon describes the recurring tendency for grocery transport to require one more trip than originally anticipated.

The discrepancy appears to arise from optimistic estimates of carrying capacity, incomplete awareness of the items to be moved, and practical obstacles encountered between the vehicle and the household.

The authors recommend that future grocery-unloading plans include a small allowance for forgotten items, doors, and the possibility that bags have weight.

However, the phrase "I'll get it all in one trip" is expected to remain in widespread use.

Further research should investigate whether making an extra trip in advance can prevent the need for one more trip later.
