## Abstract

Individuals standing in queues frequently experience the perception that adjacent queues are moving faster.

This perception often leads to queue switching.

The present paper examines the mathematical and psychological consequences of this decision.

A preliminary model is proposed in which the apparent velocity of neighbouring queues increases immediately following the abandonment of one's original queue.

The findings suggest that queue switching may constitute a self-defeating optimisation strategy.

## Introduction

The queue is one of the fundamental organisational structures of modern society.

It allows individuals to wait in an orderly fashion for services, products, transport, or access to something that several other people also want.

In principle, the queue is simple.

In practice, it generates profound emotional distress.

A particularly persistent phenomenon occurs when two or more queues are adjacent.

The individual selects one queue.

The other queue moves.

The individual observes this.

The individual changes queues.

Their original queue then moves.

This sequence has been observed repeatedly across supermarkets, airports, cafés, banks, and apparently every passport office on Earth.

## The Basic Problem

Consider two queues:

**Queue A:** the queue you are currently standing in.

**Queue B:** the other queue.

Initially, Queue A appears stationary while Queue B advances.

The individual concludes that Queue B is faster.

They switch.

Almost immediately, Queue A advances.

The individual is now standing in Queue B while watching the people they previously stood behind move toward the cashier.

This event produces a measurable decline in morale.

## A Mathematical Model

Let:

- \(A(t)\) represent the movement of Queue A.
- \(B(t)\) represent the movement of Queue B.
- \(S\) represent the moment at which the participant switches queues.

The conventional assumption is that the participant chooses the queue with the greater expected velocity.

However, observed behaviour suggests that the act of switching may itself alter the participant's perception of queue velocity.

We therefore propose:

\[
P(A > B) \rightarrow P(B > A)
\]

immediately following the decision to switch.

In plain language:

**The other queue becomes faster as soon as you join it.**

## The Grocery Store Experiment

An observational study was conducted in a supermarket containing six checkout queues.

Twenty-three participants were observed.

Of these:

- 17 changed queues at least once.
- 12 changed queues twice.
- 5 returned to their original queue.
- 3 expressed regret.
- 1 abandoned shopping entirely.

The final participant reportedly left because they had "had enough."

Their decision is not included in the mathematical analysis.

## The Adjacent Queue Effect

The closer two queues are, the more obvious their relative movement becomes.

If Queue A moves while Queue B remains stationary, participants perceive the difference immediately.

This produces what may be called the **Adjacent Queue Effect**.

Interestingly, participants rarely monitor the movement of distant queues with equal attention.

This suggests that the phenomenon is partly observational.

People do not merely want to be in the fastest queue.

They want to know that they are in the fastest queue.

## The Emotional Cost of Switching

Queue switching carries psychological consequences.

A participant who changes queues assumes responsibility for the decision.

If the new queue moves faster, the decision is validated.

If the new queue moves more slowly, the participant experiences regret.

If the original queue subsequently moves rapidly, regret intensifies.

This creates a simple decision matrix:

| Outcome | Emotional Response |
|---|---|
| New queue faster | Satisfaction |
| Both queues equal | Indifference |
| New queue slower | Regret |
| Original queue immediately moves | Rage |
| Original queue finishes before new queue | Existential crisis |

The final category warrants further investigation.

## The Supermarket Principle

The data suggest a general rule:

> The queue you are not standing in is moving faster.

This principle appears robust across multiple environments.

The authors caution, however, that the rule should not be interpreted literally.

It is possible that the queues are moving at approximately equal rates and that human attention is simply biased toward the queue containing the outcome we did not choose.

This explanation is less entertaining.

## Conclusion

Queue switching appears to offer limited benefits and substantial psychological risk.

The authors recommend remaining in the original queue unless there is strong evidence that another queue will produce a meaningful reduction in waiting time.

In all other cases, patience is advised.

The authors further recommend pretending not to notice when the adjacent queue moves faster.

This strategy does not improve one's position but may preserve dignity.
