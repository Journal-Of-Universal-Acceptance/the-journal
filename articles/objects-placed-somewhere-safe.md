## Abstract

Individuals frequently place important objects somewhere “safe.”

This action is generally intended to prevent the object from being lost.

However, objects placed somewhere safe are subsequently difficult to locate with surprising frequency.

The present study investigates this phenomenon and considers whether the concept of safety may conflict with the practical requirements of future retrieval.

Observations suggest that objects placed somewhere safe are often stored in locations that are secure, sensible, and immediately forgotten.

The authors conclude that an object's optimal storage location may depend less on how well it is protected and more on whether its future location can be remembered.

## Introduction

People lose things.

This is generally considered undesirable.

In response, individuals sometimes attempt to prevent loss by deliberately placing an important object somewhere safe.

The decision is usually accompanied by confidence.

“It'll be safe here.”

“I'll put it somewhere I won't lose it.”

“I'll remember where I put it.”

These statements are often made at the moment of storage.

Later, the object cannot be found.

The present study examines why this occurs.

## Methodology

Objects reported as missing were investigated by reconstructing the circumstances under which they had originally been stored.

Participants were asked where they believed the object had been placed.

Responses included:

- “Somewhere safe.”
- “I put it somewhere obvious.”
- “I definitely remember putting it somewhere.”
- “I don't remember exactly where.”
- “I know it's in the house.”
- “It has to be here.”

Locations were then searched.

Searches were initially conducted in obvious locations before progressing to increasingly unlikely locations.

## Results

A consistent relationship was observed between perceived safety and subsequent difficulty of retrieval.

Objects placed in ordinary locations remained relatively accessible.

Objects placed in locations described as particularly safe were significantly more difficult to locate.

### The Safe Location

The safe location was often selected because it appeared unlikely that the object would be disturbed.

This was considered beneficial.

However, the same characteristic made the location less likely to be checked during a future search.

An object placed on a desk could be found on the desk.

An object placed inside a drawer could be found in the drawer.

An object placed inside a smaller container within the drawer could be found only if the container was remembered.

An object placed somewhere inside another object could require considerably more information.

At some point, safety appeared to become concealment.

### Confidence

Participants demonstrated high confidence immediately after storing an object.

This confidence declined over time.

The statement:

> “I'll remember where I put it.”

was frequently followed by:

> “Why would I put it there?”

This suggests that memory confidence at the time of storage may not accurately predict later recall.

### The Logic of the Search

Once an object had been declared missing, participants frequently searched locations where the object would normally be expected.

The safe location was often excluded because it was not remembered as the relevant location.

This produced a paradox.

The object was difficult to find precisely because it had been stored successfully.

## Discussion

The findings suggest that safety and accessibility may represent different properties.

An object can be highly secure while being poorly accessible.

This distinction is not always considered when objects are stored.

The individual is generally concerned with preventing loss at the moment of storage.

The future individual is concerned with retrieval.

Both individuals are technically the same person.

They do not always have the same information.

The present study therefore proposes a distinction between **physical safety** and **cognitive safety**.

Physical safety refers to the likelihood that an object will remain where it was placed.

Cognitive safety refers to the likelihood that the person will remember where it was placed.

An ideal storage location would satisfy both conditions.

Such locations appear to be uncommon.

## Conclusion

Objects placed somewhere safe are not necessarily lost.

They are often exactly where they were intended to be.

The difficulty is that the person who placed them there may no longer possess sufficient information to retrieve them.

The authors recommend that important objects be stored in locations that are both secure and memorable.

Writing down the location may be beneficial.

However, the note describing the location should itself be stored somewhere memorable.

The authors acknowledge that this creates a secondary storage problem.

Further research is recommended into where people place notes reminding them where they put things.
