## Abstract

Households frequently contain more mugs than are required for the immediate consumption of beverages.

Despite this abundance, individuals often select one particular mug repeatedly.

The present study investigates why a household may contain twelve mugs while the same person consistently uses one particular mug.

The findings suggest that selection is influenced by familiarity, size, shape, handle comfort, capacity, appearance, and proximity.

However, these factors do not fully explain the phenomenon.

In particular, participants were frequently unable to explain why one mug had become the preferred mug.

The authors therefore conclude that mug ownership and mug utilisation are separate phenomena.

## Introduction

A mug is a relatively simple object.

It contains a beverage.

A person holds the mug.

The person drinks the beverage.

The mug is then placed somewhere.

The process is straightforward.

Nevertheless, households frequently contain large collections of mugs.

Some mugs may have been purchased deliberately.

Others may have been acquired as gifts.

Some may have originated from workplaces, holidays, events, or unknown sources.

Despite this variety, individuals frequently develop a preference for one particular mug.

This preference may remain stable for extended periods.

The person may own twelve mugs.

They may use one.

## Methodology

Households containing multiple mugs were observed during ordinary beverage preparation.

Participants were asked to select a mug without being given any instructions regarding which mug to choose.

The selected mug was recorded.

Participants were then asked why it had been selected.

Common responses included:

- “It's my favourite.”
- “It's the right size.”
- “I like the handle.”
- “This one holds more.”
- “It's just the one I use.”
- “I don't know.”

The final response was considered particularly significant.

Additional observations were conducted after deliberately placing the preferred mug in a different cupboard.

Participants were then observed selecting another mug while displaying mild dissatisfaction with the available alternatives.

## Results

Participants demonstrated strong consistency in mug selection.

The presence of alternative mugs did not necessarily reduce the probability of selecting the preferred mug.

In several observations, participants appeared to identify the preferred mug immediately.

No systematic inspection of all available mugs was required.

The preferred mug appeared to be known in advance.

### Capacity

Mug capacity was frequently mentioned as a reason for preference.

Participants sometimes preferred a larger mug because it reduced the need for refills.

However, capacity did not fully explain the preference.

A larger mug was not automatically selected when it was available.

This suggested that capacity was a contributing factor rather than a complete explanation.

### Handle Configuration

Participants frequently referred to the handle.

The handle was described as comfortable, familiar, or simply “right.”

Attempts to determine what constituted a correct handle were unsuccessful.

Measurements of handle size did not consistently predict preference.

This suggests that handle comfort may be partly objective and partly learned.

### Familiarity

Repeated use appeared to strengthen the status of a mug.

Once a mug had been used successfully several times, participants became increasingly likely to select it again.

The mug had effectively entered a routine.

At this point, selecting another mug required a reason.

Selecting the usual mug did not.

### Ownership

Ownership appeared to have a significant effect.

Participants sometimes described a mug as “mine” even when the mug was technically shared by the household.

In some cases, the basis of this ownership was unclear.

The participant had not purchased the mug.

No formal ownership agreement existed.

Nevertheless, the mug had become associated with that person.

Attempts by another household member to use the mug occasionally resulted in immediate recognition.

The mug had therefore acquired an informal property status.

## Discussion

The findings suggest that mug selection is not determined solely by practical considerations.

If practical considerations were the only factor, individuals would be expected to select the mug that was most suitable for the beverage being consumed.

This does not appear to occur consistently.

Instead, people develop routines around particular mugs.

The preferred mug becomes familiar.

Its location becomes known.

Its handle feels correct.

Its capacity is remembered.

Its appearance becomes associated with the act of having a drink.

Eventually, the individual may stop making an active decision.

The mug is simply selected.

This raises an important question.

Why does the household continue to require twelve mugs?

The study found no satisfactory answer.

Additional mugs remained present despite being selected infrequently.

They occupied cupboards.

They were occasionally washed.

They were returned to cupboards.

They waited.

Meanwhile, the preferred mug continued to be used.

## The Missing Mug Effect

A particularly interesting result occurred when the preferred mug was unavailable.

Participants did not immediately choose the second-best mug.

Instead, several participants searched for the preferred mug.

This behaviour was notable because alternative mugs were visibly available.

The search therefore appeared to be motivated not by the need for a mug, but by the need for **that mug**.

Once the preferred mug was located, normal behaviour resumed.

If it could not be located, the participant selected another mug with reduced enthusiasm.

## Conclusion

The Mug Ownership Problem describes the apparent contradiction between the number of mugs owned by a household and the number of mugs actually preferred by its occupants.

The study suggests that mug preference develops through a combination of familiarity, routine, physical characteristics, and informal ownership.

However, these factors do not fully explain why one mug becomes the mug.

The authors therefore recommend further research into the moment at which a mug ceases to be one of several available mugs and becomes personally significant.

Future studies should also investigate why households continue acquiring additional mugs when the existing mugs are already sufficient.

The possibility that the mugs themselves are responsible for this behaviour cannot yet be excluded.
