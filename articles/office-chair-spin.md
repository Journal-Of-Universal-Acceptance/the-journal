## Abstract

The office chair is commonly understood as a device intended to facilitate seated work.

This interpretation is incomplete.

Modern office chairs permit rotation, and this rotational capacity has generated a diverse range of behaviours that remain inadequately classified in contemporary scholarship.

The present study establishes a preliminary taxonomy of office-chair spinning based upon duration, velocity, motivation, and degree of regret.

Seven principal categories are identified.

The author further argues that office-chair rotation represents an underappreciated form of workplace movement.

## Introduction

The office chair occupies an unusual position within the modern workplace.

It is simultaneously furniture, equipment, transportation device, and occasional source of mild danger.

Unlike the ordinary chair, which maintains a stable relationship between the user and the surrounding environment, the office chair permits the user to rotate freely while remaining approximately where they were.

This capability raises several questions.

Why do people spin?

How much spinning is too much?

And why, after spinning several times, does everyone appear surprised that they are dizzy?

The present paper attempts to provide answers.

## Methodology

The study was conducted in three offices, one home workspace, and a meeting room in which the researcher was explicitly asked not to spin.

Observations were categorised according to:

- number of rotations;
- apparent intention;
- duration;
- speed;
- resulting facial expression;
- and whether the participant subsequently claimed they were "just testing it."

A total of 126 rotational events were recorded.

## A Proposed Taxonomy

### Type I: The Adjustment Spin

The Adjustment Spin involves a rotation of less than 90 degrees and is generally performed for legitimate workplace purposes.

Typical examples include turning toward a colleague or reaching for an adjacent monitor.

No significant risk is associated with this category.

The Adjustment Spin is therefore excluded from further consideration.

### Type II: The Casual Spin

The Casual Spin involves one complete rotation without obvious practical purpose.

Participants frequently claimed that they had not intended to spin.

This claim was rarely credible.

### Type III: The Boredom Rotation

The Boredom Rotation occurs during periods of low productivity.

It generally involves repeated rotations while the participant is reading an email, attending a video meeting, or pretending to attend a video meeting.

This category appears particularly common on Friday afternoons.

### Type IV: The Momentum Event

The Momentum Event begins as an ordinary rotation but continues beyond the point originally intended.

The participant discovers that the chair has momentum.

The participant then discovers that momentum is difficult to negotiate with.

### Type V: The Experimental Spin

The Experimental Spin begins with the explicit intention to determine how many rotations the chair can complete.

No useful information is generally obtained.

### Type VI: The Regret Spiral

The Regret Spiral occurs when the participant has exceeded their preferred number of rotations and begins attempting to stop.

Stopping the chair produces another rotation.

The participant attempts to compensate.

The chair rotates again.

The cycle continues until equilibrium is restored or someone walks past and asks whether everything is alright.

### Type VII: The Incident

The Incident is defined as any rotational event followed by the words:

> "I'm never doing that again."

The declaration is generally temporary.

## Results

The most significant finding was the relationship between confidence and rotational stability.

Participants who believed they were capable of controlling a spinning office chair were significantly more likely to spin beyond their intended stopping point.

This suggests that confidence may be inversely related to rotational competence.

The study also found that no participant successfully calculated their maximum comfortable rotation threshold in advance.

## Discussion

Office-chair rotation appears to serve several psychological functions.

It may relieve boredom.

It may provide sensory stimulation.

It may provide an alternative to completing one's assigned work.

However, the present study proposes an additional explanation: people spin because they can.

The existence of a rotational mechanism creates an irresistible possibility.

The chair asks a question.

The human answers by spinning.

## Conclusion

The office chair should no longer be regarded as merely a passive workplace object.

Its rotational capacity produces a complex range of behaviours worthy of further study.

The present taxonomy provides a foundation for future research.

Further investigation should examine:

1. The relationship between chair colour and rotational frequency.
2. Whether leather chairs produce more confident spinning.
3. Why people spin more when nobody is watching.
4. The relationship between spinning and procrastination.
5. Whether a chair can be considered a vehicle.

The author recommends that all future office chairs be fitted with a small warning label reading:

**ROTATION MAY HAVE CONSEQUENCES.**
