## Abstract

An elevator call button is designed to communicate a request.

A person presses the button.

The button illuminates.

The elevator is expected to arrive.

Under these conditions, a second press would appear unnecessary.

Nevertheless, people frequently press an already illuminated button.

Some press it once more.

Others press it several times.

A person who has watched someone else press the button may also press it.

The present study examines this behaviour and the possible distinction between requesting an elevator and expressing an opinion about how long the elevator is taking.

The findings suggest that the first press is an instruction, while subsequent presses are increasingly a form of commentary.

## Introduction

Waiting for an elevator involves a limited number of available activities.

A person may look at the floor indicator.

They may look at their phone.

They may examine the doors.

They may stand quietly.

They may press the call button.

The last activity has a clear practical purpose when the button has not yet been pressed.

Once the button is illuminated, however, the purpose becomes less obvious.

The system has acknowledged the request.

There is no indication that it has forgotten.

Yet the button remains available.

The waiting person can press it again.

And often does.

This study investigates why an acknowledged request is repeatedly submitted to a system that has provided no opportunity for negotiation.

## Methodology

Ordinary elevator-waiting situations were considered in which a call button was visibly illuminated before a further press occurred.

The investigation distinguished between four kinds of interaction:

- the initial press, which submits a request for an elevator;
- the confirmatory press, which occurs after the button has already illuminated;
- the impatient press, which follows a period of waiting judged excessive by the person waiting;
- and the collective press, in which a new arrival presses a button that another person has already pressed.

Particular attention was given to the relationship between the visible state of the button and the apparent expectations of the person pressing it.

The study did not attempt to alter elevator control software.

It also did not attempt to establish whether the elevator was travelling slowly, stopping at other floors, or simply taking a perfectly reasonable amount of time.

These distinctions were considered unlikely to influence the waiting person's immediate assessment.

## Results

Several recurring patterns were identified.

### The Illuminated Button

The illuminated button was generally interpreted as evidence that a request had been received.

This interpretation was especially strong immediately after the first press.

As waiting continued, however, the same light appeared to become less reassuring.

The button had not changed.

Its meaning had.

At first, the light indicated that the elevator was coming.

Later, it indicated that the elevator had not yet come.

The same visual signal therefore supported two different emotional conclusions.

### The Confirmatory Press

A second press often occurred without any visible change in the system.

The light remained on.

The elevator did not immediately appear.

The person resumed waiting.

From a mechanical perspective, the second press appeared to have accomplished nothing.

From a behavioural perspective, it had accomplished an action.

The individual had done something about the wait.

This distinction may explain why the behaviour persists despite the absence of obvious evidence that it works.

### The Escalation Sequence

Repeated pressing sometimes increased in frequency as waiting continued.

A single additional press could become two presses.

Two presses could become a short sequence.

The button was then released and the doors were watched closely.

The sequence suggested an implicit theory of elevator operation in which a request becomes more persuasive through repetition.

No evidence was found that the elevator had agreed to this arrangement.

### The New Arrival

A particularly informative situation occurred when a second person arrived at an elevator already being awaited.

The first person stood beside an illuminated button.

The second person observed the button.

The second person pressed it.

This action could not be explained solely by uncertainty about whether the elevator had been called.

The button was illuminated.

The first person was waiting.

The doors were closed.

The available evidence was considerable.

Nevertheless, the new arrival appeared to require an independent contribution to the process.

The researchers propose that pressing the button may serve as a declaration of participation.

The first person had requested an elevator.

The second person wished to be recorded as also wanting one.

### The Arrival Attribution Problem

When the elevator eventually arrived after several additional presses, the final press sometimes appeared to receive informal credit.

This presented a difficulty.

The elevator might have been on its way since the first press.

It might have arrived at exactly the same moment if everyone had stood perfectly still.

Because the alternative outcome could not be observed, the apparent success of the final press remained difficult to disprove.

The elevator arrived.

Someone had recently pressed the button.

A relationship was therefore suspected.

## Discussion

The findings suggest that elevator-button pressing serves two functions that should be considered separately.

The first is **system communication**.

A person tells the elevator that transport is required.

The second is **waiting management**.

A person performs an action to make an otherwise passive period feel more manageable.

The first function is usually completed by one press.

The second may require an unlimited number.

This distinction helps explain why an illuminated button does not prevent further interaction.

The button has already fulfilled its technical purpose, but it remains one of the few objects in the immediate environment that can be acted upon.

The doors cannot reasonably be opened by hand.

The floor indicator cannot be persuaded to advance.

The elevator shaft does not accept verbal complaints.

The button, however, can still be pressed.

It offers the comforting possibility that an action is being taken, even when the action adds no new information.

The study therefore proposes the **Repeated Request Principle**:

**When a system has acknowledged a request but has not yet fulfilled it, people may repeat the request in order to acknowledge their own continued dissatisfaction.**

This principle may apply beyond elevators.

It may help explain repeated clicks on a loading page, repeated checks of a parcel-tracking screen, and the decision to refresh an inbox immediately after confirming that no new message has arrived.

In each case, the system's state is already known.

The user nevertheless seeks a new interaction.

A further question concerns responsibility.

If a person waits without pressing the button again, the delay belongs entirely to the elevator.

If they press it again, they have at least attempted to intervene.

The intervention may have no effect.

It does, however, make the wait feel less like an activity in which the individual has been excluded from decision-making.

## Limitations

Elevator control systems vary.

Some buttons may support functions that are not visible to ordinary passengers.

A light may fail, or a request may not register.

Under such circumstances, pressing a button again could have a practical purpose.

The present study concerns the more specific situation in which the request appears to have been acknowledged and no evidence suggests otherwise.

The researchers also acknowledge that an elevator may arrive shortly after an additional press.

This timing does not establish that the additional press caused the arrival.

Unfortunately, it is often enough to encourage another press on the next occasion.

## Conclusion

The repeated pressing of an illuminated elevator button appears to be less an attempt to communicate new information than an attempt to participate in the process of waiting.

The first press requests an elevator.

The second confirms that the person still wants the elevator.

Further presses communicate that the person is becoming increasingly interested in the elevator's whereabouts.

The authors recommend that future studies investigate whether elevator waiting times would feel longer if call buttons became physically inaccessible after the first successful press.

Until then, the button is expected to remain illuminated.

And to be pressed again.
